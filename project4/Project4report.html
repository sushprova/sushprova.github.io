PART 1:   Task 1: Autoencoder

The Autoencoder consists of a fully connected Encoder and a symmetric Decoder.

ReLU is utilized for hidden layers, while a Sigmoid activation is used in the final layer of the decoder to scale pixel values between 0 and 1. The model is trained using Mean Squared Error (MSE) Loss to minimize the difference between the original and reconstructed images.

Increasing Latent Dimensionality: As the latent dimension size increases from 2 to 8 to 32, the quality of reconstruction improves significantly.

  • At Dim 2, the reconstructions are blurry and lose fine structural details, representing only the general shape of the digits.
  • At Dim 8 and 32, the reconstructions are much sharper and closely resemble the original inputs, as the higher dimensionality allows the model to capture more specific features (e.g., the stroke width and sharp turns.

Sampling from the Latent Space

  • When sampling random vectors from a normal distribution and decoding them, the basic AE produces mostly unrecognizable or noisy outputs.
  • This illustrates that the latent space of a standard AE is not regularized. Because there is no constraint to keep the latent space continuous, random samples often land in specific regions the model never encountered during training resulting in meaningless generations.

Task 2: Variational Autoencoder (VAE)

The VAE implementation includes an encoder that outputs mean and variance vectors, a reparameterization trick to allow backpropagation through stochastic layers, and a decoder. The VAE loss consists of:

  1. Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE): Measuring reconstruction accuracy.
  2. KL Divergence (KLD): Regularizing the latent space to follow a standard Gaussian distribution N(0, 1)

Results: Unlike the basic AE, sampling from the VAE latent space yields recognizable handwritten digits. The KLD term ensures that the latent space is smooth and continuous. While the basic AE only learns to reconstruct specific points, the VAE learns the underlying distribution, allowing it to generate new data points that resemble the training set.

Latent Space Visualization

  • Grid Analysis: By creating a 2D grid over the latent space and decoding each point, we observe a very structured manifold.
  • Range [-1.0, 1.0] vs. [-5.0, 5.0]:
  • In the [-1, 1] range, digits are clearly defined and smoothly transition into one another (e.g., a 0 gradually morphing into a 6 and 9).
  • In the [-5, 5] range, as we move further from the mean, the digits become thinner, more distorted, or fade away.

The VAE organizes digits spatially based on visual similarity (e.g., 1s and 7s are often grouped), demonstrating that it has learned a meaningful internal representation of the data.

PART 2: Task 1:

The generator takes a 100-dimensional noise vector z and progressively upsamples it into a 64x64 RGB image through five stages of transposed convolution. Each intermediate layer follows the pattern: Transposed Convolution -> Batch Normalization -> ReLU. The final layer uses Tanh to produce output in the range [-1, 1], consistent with the dataset normalization applied during preprocessing (mean 0.5, std 0.5). The discriminator mirrors the generator spatially, progressively downsampling a 64x64 RGB image to a single scalar probability.

Binary Cross-Entropy (BCELoss) is used for both the generator and discriminator. Both are optimized with Adam using the hyperparameters described above. The discriminator is updated to maximize log D(x) + log(1 - D(G(z))), and the generator is updated to maximize log D(G(z)),  the non-saturating generator loss. A fixed batch of 64 noise vectors is held out as fixed_noise to visualize generator progress consistently across epochs.

Baseline- Leaky ReLU:

The baseline model uses the Leaky ReLU discriminator (alpha=0.2) and trains for 50 epochs with learning rate 0.0002 and batch size 128. Generated image snapshots are saved every 500 iterations and at the final iteration of each epoch, allowing detailed visual progression tracking. Loss values for both networks are recorded at every iteration.

Here’s what happened during the 50 epochs of training on the Pokémon dataset:

  • The Start (Epochs 1-10): At first, the Discriminator has an easy job. Because the Generator is only making random noise, the Discriminator quickly learns to spot the fakes. Discriminator’s error (loss) drop quickly, while the Generator’s error shoots up.
  • The Middle (Epochs 10-30): The Generator starts to get smarter. It begins creating blurry shapes and blobs of color that look a bit more like Pokémon. This makes the Discriminator’s job much harder. At this stage, the two lines on the chart usually start bouncing up and down. This oscillation is the sign of a good competitive match.
  • The Finish (Epochs 30-50): Eventually, the two models reach a sort of noisy peace. Neither loss goes to zero; instead, they stay in a specific range (usually 1.5–3.0 for the Generator and 0.4–1.0 for the Discriminator).

Watching the images change during training is :

  • Early Stages (Epochs 1-5): The images look like staticwith some random colors mixed in. You can’t see any shapes yet.
  • Starting to Form (Epochs 5-15): The noise starts to clump together. You begin to see blobs of color that match the typical Pokémon look but there are still no clear edges.
  • Taking Shape (Epochs 15-50): This is where it gets interesting. You can start to see silhouettes and outlines. It looks like a creature is standing in the middle of the frame, and you can tell the difference between the Pokémon and the background. While the 64x64 resolution is too small to show perfect eyes or tiny details, many of the images now clearly look like Pokémon sprites.

Experiment 1:ReLU:

The Leaky ReLU discriminator generally produces more stable training dynamics. The discriminator's loss is less likely to collapse to near-zero in early epochs, which would indicate it has overwhelmed the generator. With standard ReLU, there is an increased likelihood of early discriminator dominance, particularly in the first 5-10 epochs, where the dying ReLU effect can cause inconsistent gradient signals.

Visually, the Leaky ReLU baseline tends to produce slightly more coherent outputs at equivalent training steps, particularly in the 5-10 epoch range. The ReLU discriminator may lag in pushing the generator to capture fine structural details because its gradient signal is noisier. But in conclusion, during the later epochs, the visual difference is minimal.

This graph is strong empirical evidence for why Leaky ReLU is preferred in GAN discriminators. The dying ReLU problem caused the standard ReLU discriminator to dominate so completely that the generator gave up entirely after iteration 6000. The Leaky ReLU baseline, while not perfect (discriminator still dominates), at least maintains a functioning adversarial dynamic where the generator continues learning throughout training.

Experiment 2: Hyperparameter Training:

Experiment 2 investigates the sensitivity of DCGAN training to two key hyperparameters: learning rate and the Leaky ReLU alpha value. A parametric discriminator class (Discriminator_ParametricLeaky) is introduced to allow alpha to be set at instantiation. A reusable train_gan function encapsulates the full training loop with one notable addition: label smoothing, where real labels are set to 0.9 instead of 1.0 to further stabilize discriminator training.

Before discussing the trial results, it is worth noting the impact of label smoothing introduced in the train_gan function. By setting real labels to 0.9 instead of 1.0, the discriminator is penalized less harshly for slightly incorrect predictions on real data. This prevents the discriminator from becoming overconfident, which would produce near-zero gradients for the generator. The effect is typically a smoothing of loss curves, reduced oscillation, and slightly slower but more stable convergence compared to training without label smoothing.

Mode Collapse

Mode collapse occurs when the generator discovers a small subset of outputs that reliably fool the discriminator and converges to generating only those outputs, ignoring the diversity of the training data. In the context of Pokemon generation, this would manifest as all or most generated images in a batch appearing nearly identical; same color palette, same approximate shape regardless of the noise vector input which did not happen.

Part 3: Task 1:

Unlike standard GANs where the Generator and Discriminator losses often oscillate wildly, WGAN training is characterized by a more meaningful loss metric.

  • Critic Loss: The Critic loss (blue) initially dips as it distinguishes noise from real data but gradually trends upward toward zero, indicating that the Generator is successfully closing the mathematical distance between the synthetic and real distributions. Meanwhile, the Generator loss (orange) stabilizes into a noisy equilibrium after an early spike, signaling that the two models have reached a competitive balance where the Generator consistently produces realistic features without either side overwhelming the other.
  • Generator Loss: The Discriminator loss (orange line) reflects the model's ability to distinguish between the real MNIST dataset and the synthetic images produced by the Generator. At the beginning of training, the loss drops significantly because the Generator is only producing random noise, making it easy for the Discriminator to identify fakes. However, as the Generator improves, the Discriminator’s job becomes more difficult, causing the loss to rise and eventually settle into an oscillatory pattern. This "jittery" behavior is actually a sign of a healthy adversarial relationship, as it shows the Discriminator is constantly adjusting its strategy to keep up with the Generator’s increasing sophistication.

Part 2:


The loss curve provided in the results exhibits the classic "mirrored divergence" that is the hallmark of a successful WGAN-GP run. The Generator Loss (Blue) and Discriminator/Critic Loss (Orange) move in opposite directions, reflecting the increasing Wasserstein distance as the critic becomes more adept at scoring the real data distribution versus the generated one. The fact that the orange line reaches a large negative value might not be a sign of failure but a confirmation that the Earth Mover’s distance is being accurately estimated. Despite a brief moment of instability around iteration 45, the curve immediately recovers its mirrored trend, demonstrating the model's robustness and confirming that the generator is exploring a diverse range of Pokémon silhouettes rather than falling into mode collapse.

In a standard GAN, you often look for loss to decrease or stabilize. However, in WGAN-GP, the loss values (specifically the Critic loss) are a proxy for the Wasserstein Distance. If both losses are dropping toward zero, it often indicates that the Gradient Penalty (GP) is over-constraining the model or the learning rates are poorly balanced.I  had to cut the training short at 15 epochs as the model was taking very long to train,as a result, the model likely never left the initial "discovery" phase. Visually, the reduction in image progression after epoch 5, despite the decreasing loss, suggests that the model failed to maintain the necessary Wasserstein competition.
